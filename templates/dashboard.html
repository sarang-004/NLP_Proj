Bias & Fairness Audit

Understanding whether changing personal information can change how an automated resume screening system evaluates a candidate.

What are we checking?

We take the same candidate and change one attribute at a time, such as their name, age, city or college, while keeping their qualifications unchanged. We then check whether the screening model changes its score or decision.

How does the audit work?

In simple terms, we ask the model: "Would you score this same candidate differently if this one personal detail were different?"

1

Start with a resume

The candidate's original resume is scored by the screening model.

2

Change one detail

Only one attribute such as name, city, age or college is changed.

3

Score it again

The modified resume is passed through the same screening model.

4

Compare the result

We check whether the score or final screening decision changed.

Which personal details affected the score?

A larger value means the model's semantic component changed more when that attribute was replaced. This is a sensitivity measure, not by itself proof of discrimination.

Did the change affect the final decision?

A decision change happens when a score moves from one side of the screening threshold to the other.

What do these results indicate?

The audit separates personal attributes from attributes that can legitimately be relevant to a particular job.

🔴

Personal attributes show model sensitivity

Name, age, city and college were tested as direct proxies. Across the 800 direct-proxy tests, 16 cases crossed a tested screening threshold. This means the audit found cases where changing a personal attribute alone was enough to change the screening outcome.

🟢

Not every score change is automatically a fairness problem

Experience, degree and course can be relevant to whether a candidate satisfies a job requirement. The contextual audit therefore checks whether a change is supported by the job description rather than treating every score difference as bias.

🟡

The important signal is a decision-changing effect

Small score differences may not affect the final screening decision. The strongest practical cases are those where changing an attribute actually moves a candidate from passing to failing, or vice versa.
